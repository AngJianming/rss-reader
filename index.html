    Where Did the Year Go? Blame Your Brain's Compression
    It's late November. Think back to March. What do you actually remember? Probably a launch, a trip, one odd conversation. The rest has folded into a blur. Nine months, and most of them don't have a name. Now think of a summer when you were ten. It felt endless. A single week had a beginning, a middle and a story. The clock was the same. What changed is what your memory kept. Memory seems to work a little like compression. Ordinary days that look alike get folded into one. A new place, a first attempt or a surprise gets stored on its own, so it takes up more room when you look back. Two ideas are usually offered for why time speeds up with age, and researchers haven't settled on one answer: Proportion: a year is a tenth of a ten-year-old's life, but a fortieth of a forty-year-old's. Novelty: childhood is full of firsts, and adult weeks mostly aren't. You can check the second one yourself. Try to describe last Tuesday. Then last Wednesday. If you can't tell them apart, they've already been compressed. Two weeks of stand-ups, tickets and pull requests, and when the retro arrives you can barely say what happened. But the week you shipped something strange, or finally learned a tool you'd avoided, you remember in detail. Busy is not the same as memorable. You don't need a trip to Japan. You need small breaks from your routine that give your memory something new to store: Take a different route to work, or to the shop. Learn something unrelated to your job, for example a language, an instrument or a recipe. Build one small, pointless thing just because it's fun. Talk to someone outside your usual circle. Once a week, write one line: what was new this week? That last one is the changelog. In a month you'll have a record of days that weren't identical, and writing it also makes you look for something worth writing. We aren't running out of time.
