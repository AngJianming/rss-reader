    Sustained Focus Depends on Returning, Not Perfection
    Maintaining focus is often treated as a test of willpower: keep your eyes on the task and refuse every interruption. That standard sounds admirable, but it confuses uninterrupted attention with sustained progress. Real work includes small disruptions—a question from a colleague, a file that needs locating, a thought about an errand. The useful question is not whether attention ever moves. It is how reliably you can return. Imagine drafting a report at a shared desk. You reach a difficult paragraph, pause to check one source, answer a quick question, then come back to a blinking cursor. If you judge the session only by its interruptions, it looks like failure. Yet the report may be moving forward. Conversely, sitting still for an hour while rereading the same sentence can look focused without producing much. Before stepping away, leave a short breadcrumb: the question you were answering, the next action, or the sentence you planned to test. It need not become a tracking system. A note such as “compare these two examples, then write the contrast” can spare you from reconstructing the whole mental route when you return. For difficult work, I would protect the first stretch from optional interruptions, then judge the session by how often I regained the thread and what I advanced.
